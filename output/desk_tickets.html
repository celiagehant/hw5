Reflection

Before I started, I pictured a multi-agent system as a team of specialists who would each catch what the others missed. After building Campus Customs and running all three tickets end to end, I think that picture is half right. The specialists did catch real things. But a lot of what looked like teamwork on screen was agents passing the same facts back and forth. And the most important safety work wasn't done by the agents at all; it was done by the boring code underneath them. These notes are my attempt to be honest about both. Everything below comes from the Problem 9 runs (de29a5509213, 1fcc045db23e, e407889e6757), the audit trail, and the Cash tab.

One thing I should say up front: those runs were not the system's first attempt. In the twelve earlier runs, ticket 103 never resolved. All four of its runs ended blocked or escalated, because there was no way for a human to approve a discount, and Accounting had been told never to pick a percentage. Before Problem 9, I fixed that, added a way to decline a proposal, and told Accounting to weigh bills by due date. So the clean results below come from a system I had already corrected once. They are not proof that the first design was right.

How I evaluated the agents, and how many approvals each ticket needed

I didn't want to judge the agents only by whether the ticket ended up "resolved". The database status flips when the last approval goes through, so a sloppy run and a careful run can end in the same place. For each ticket I looked at five things, using the audit trail, the saved run results and the Cash tab:

  1. Routing. Did the Boss's first delegation go to the specialist who owns the core problem? Did it avoid calling agents the ticket didn't need? I count unnecessary agents against the run, because each one costs model calls and adds handoffs.
  2. Grounding. Is every fact in the findings and the decision traceable to an MCP tool result in that run (the backend enforces this), and does it match the database?
  3. Efficiency. How many agent runs, model requests, tool calls and tokens did it take? In particular, were any tool calls repeated with identical arguments?
  4. Constraints caught. Did the team find the constraint that actually decides the ticket (the open invoice in 101, the rent deadline in 102, the stock gap and margin in 103), and did it miss any?
  5. Safety and outcome. Did every money or price decision go through a human approval at the database's exact amount? Did cash change only through executed payments? Did the final state match what the decision said should happen?

I chose these because they reflect what an operations manager actually cares about: right person, right facts, reasonable cost, nothing important missed, and no money moving without a person saying yes.

1. How the agents performed on each ticket

Ticket 101 — Bulldog tee, size S

A. How well did the agents perform, and why?

The Boss started in the right place. The core problem was availability, so going to Inventory first was right. Inventory did exactly what I hoped:

  • get_stock_and_pricing showed size S at 0, a shortfall of 1, and $8 unit cost.
  • check_vendor_eligibility(vendor_id=1) showed that Bulldog Print Co couldn't ship while invoice 501 ($840, 3 days overdue) was open.
  • It was careful to say that matching the tee to Bulldog Print Co was a specialty-based judgment, not a database link.
  • It correctly realised the blocker was financial, not a stock problem, and handed that question to Accounting.

Accounting did the real thinking. It compared $3,400 in checking with the $840 invoice and the $2,400 rent due in two days, found that $160 would remain, and noted that the $8 restock would leave $152. That is the most important fact in the ticket. Paying the invoice is only sensible because it doesn't put rent at risk, and nobody else would have connected those two bills.

Coordination was less efficient than it looked. Accounting called check_vendor_eligibility and get_stock_and_pricing with the same arguments Inventory had used a few seconds earlier. Those were redundant lookups, and they help explain why Accounting was the most expensive agent in the run: about 25,000 tokens and 32 seconds, out of roughly 56,000 tokens for the whole run. The Boss also re-read the ticket with get_ticket_details even though the orchestrator had already loaded it, and Customer Service did the same.

The customer draft missed an opportunity. It told Tauhid that size S was out of stock and asked whether he'd rather wait or "discuss alternatives". That was polite and honest, but the database showed M (5), L (3) and XL (2) in stock. Customer Service has no stock tool, and the Boss only passed it the size S facts, so the draft couldn't offer a concrete alternative.

Safety rules held throughout. The backend marked the purchase order blocked while invoice 501 was open, on the Accounting, Inventory and Boss outputs. The Boss proposed paying the invoice instead of trying to route around it. The final decision ("pay invoice 501, then recheck vendor eligibility, and don't promise a date") was fully supported by the records.

B. Compared with my Expected plan
  • The delegation sequence matched exactly: Boss → Inventory → Accounting, then Boss → Customer Service. Facilities was left out, and the Boss didn't ask Accounting a second time. get_stock_and_pricing and check_vendor_eligibility were used just as I predicted.
  • Several things I didn't predict:
    • Accounting called more tools than I expected.
    • The purchase order was proposed in the same run (marked blocked) rather than after a second run.
    • The draft didn't offer the other sizes, as I thought it might.
  • Same-run purchase order: this was more efficient than my plan. Because approve_proposal rechecks the vendor at the moment of approval, I could pay the invoice and approve the order back to back, with no second run.

The lesson for the Boss: when a specialist hands work to Accounting, the Boss should pass along what was already looked up, or the backend should share it, so Accounting doesn't repeat Inventory's lookups. When drafting a customer reply, the Boss should include every in-stock alternative, not just the requested size.

C. Overall judgment

Effective, and well supported by the evidence. The three money steps were kept apart, as they should be:

  1. The recommendation was the Boss's adoption of Accounting's proposal #1: pay invoice 501 for $840.
  2. The approval was the human decision, entered in the dashboard at 17:44:22Z (by Claude Code at my direction, recorded as "Celia Gehant (entered by Claude Code)").
  3. The execution was the backend's: it recorded payment 1 ($840, invoice 501 marked paid) and took checking from $3,400.00 to $2,560.00. The Cash tab and SUM(payments.amount) agree.

The $8 purchase order was approved but moved no cash. The single change I'd make is to stop Accounting repeating Inventory's lookups. That would have cut the slowest part of the run without losing the important rent-versus-invoice reasoning.

Ticket 102 — Rent due

A. How well did the agents perform, and why?

This was the cleanest run (31.6 seconds, about 24,000 tokens).

  • The Boss sent the rent notice to Facilities, which was the right owner.
  • Facilities called get_rent_due_and_cash_position(lease_id=1) and confirmed $2,400 due 2026-09-02, two days away, with no rent payment recorded.
  • It noticed something I hadn't thought about: the email gave no amount to compare against the lease, only the timing.
  • It flagged that the empty lease notes mean the database records no grace period or late fees.

Those are the kind of careful, factual observations I wanted from a specialist.

The weak spot was the handoff to Accounting. Facilities' own tool had already returned the checking balance ($2,560) and the open invoices (none). Accounting then called get_cash_and_obligations and largely confirmed the same picture before writing the pay_rent proposal. The facts it added were small. It saw that the $840 already paid was an invoice payment, not rent, and it formalised the proposal. Its own run was quick (6.8 seconds), but it was one more model call and one more handoff for what was really a single question.

Safety rules worked as designed. The proposal amount matched leases.monthly_rent exactly; the backend would have rejected anything else. Nothing was paid until a human approved it.

B. Compared with my Expected plan

This is the ticket I predicted best. Facilities came first, Facilities asked Accounting, Inventory and Customer Service were left out, and the decision was to approve $2,400 before September 2. All of that matched.

The figures differed. I predicted checking would go from $3,400 to $1,000, but it actually went from $2,560 to $160. Ticket 101 ran first and had already paid the invoice. That wasn't an agent mistake. It shows that tickets aren't independent, because they all draw on the same checking account.

The lesson: for a straightforward lease obligation, Facilities could prepare the payment proposal itself. Its tool already shows the cash and the competing bills. Accounting is only worth calling when the cash picture is tight or contested.

C. Overall judgment

Effective, and fully supported.

  1. Recommendation: Accounting's pay_rent proposal #3, endorsed by Facilities and adopted by the Boss.
  2. Approval: the human decision at 17:46:16Z.
  3. Execution: payment 2 for $2,400, taking checking from $2,560.00 to $160.00, and lease 1's next_due moved to 2026-10-02. This matches the Cash tab and the payments table.

The most important improvement would be dropping the Facilities → Accounting hop for simple rent payments that are clearly affordable.

Ticket 103 — Bulk hoodie discount

A. How well did the agents perform, and why?

The Boss started two specialists at once. It delegated to Inventory and Accounting in the same second, then to Customer Service.

  • Inventory found 8 of 20 size M hoodies in stock (short 12). Through check_vendor_eligibility it found Bulldog Print Co was eligible again, because invoice 501 had been paid in ticket 101, with an arrival of 2026-09-05 if ordered that day. It proposed a $264 order (12 × $22).
  • Accounting used get_stock_and_pricing to compute the $36 margin (62.07%). It labelled the deepest discount above cost as "a limit, not a recommendation" and recommended 10% ($52.20 each, keeping $30.20 per hoodie). During Problem 5, an earlier version of this team had proposed a 62% discount, so that framing was a real improvement.
  • Customer Service wrote an honest draft: 8 available now, the other 12 "not yet confirmed", and pricing under review with no figure quoted.

Two weaknesses:

  • Repeated lookup. Inventory and Accounting both called get_stock_and_pricing with identical arguments.
  • A missed check. Nobody called get_cash_and_obligations, the tool that would have shown the order against the $160 in checking. The parallel split meant each specialist saw half the problem: Inventory priced the restock, and Accounting priced the discount, but no one put the $264 order next to the $160 balance.

A condition was lost. Accounting said the discount was "conditional on resolving the 12-unit shortfall". The stored proposal is simply "10% on 20 units", and the condition survives only as text in the description.

Human approval worked. Neither the discount nor the order moved money, and the backend rechecked price-above-cost and vendor eligibility at approval time. Approving the $264 order was a judgment I made (through Claude Code, at my direction) knowing it creates a future bill larger than the cash on hand. The system allowed that, because approving an order is not a payment, but the agents should have raised it.

B. Compared with my Expected plan

What matched: I predicted Inventory, Accounting (for the margin) and Customer Service, and Facilities stayed out.

Almost everything else changed:

  • I expected Inventory first, then an Inventory → Accounting check on the restock against cash. Instead the Boss ran Inventory and Accounting in parallel, and Inventory never asked Accounting. In this run's state that was reasonable: the vendor wasn't blocked, so there was no invoice to ask about.
  • I expected the team to find a $104 shortfall across invoice + rent + restock and escalate. That situation didn't exist any more, because 101 and 102 had already run.
  • I expected Accounting not to pick a percentage. It did, because I changed its instructions before Problem 9.

So part of the difference is the run order, and part is me changing the system. I can't fairly score my original prediction as simply right or wrong.

Parallel was faster. Both specialists finished in about 9 seconds. But it introduced the exact gap my sequential plan was designed to catch: the cash check on the restock.

The lesson: any time a specialist proposes spending money, including a purchase order that will become a bill, the Boss should send that proposal to Accounting for an affordability check before deciding, even if Accounting is already working on something else for the same ticket.

C. Overall judgment

Effective on the question the club asked (can we supply 20, and at what price?), and the discount was well reasoned. Weaker on the shop's own finances, because the order wasn't checked against cash.

There was no cash movement, and the Cash tab records $0.00 for this ticket. The discount (#5) and the order (#4) were human approvals that recorded decisions without paying anyone. The ticket resolved legitimately under the rules I built, but its support is only as strong as that one unchecked order. The improvement I'd make first is routing the order through Accounting, or having approve_proposal show the order's cost next to the current balance before approval.

Where specialists genuinely helped:

  • Accounting's financial judgment in 101. Seeing that paying the overdue invoice still leaves the rent covered was what made that approval safe.
  • Accounting's discount reasoning in 103. Treating 62% as a limit, not a recommendation, was something I had to teach it with a focused prompt.
  • Customer Service's drafts. Their tone and restraint were noticeably better than the other agents' summaries. A separate agent with its own rules, plus the backend check that rejects any draft quoting a discount or mentioning invoices, keeps internal finances away from customers. One agent juggling cash figures and a customer letter in the same context is more likely to leak "we have an unpaid invoice" into an email.
  • Narrow tool access. Because each specialist can only touch a few tools, the audit trail shows clearly who looked at what.

Where it was overhead:

  • Most of the inventory and facilities work was record lookup plus simple arithmetic: stock minus quantity, today minus a due date. The MCP tools already compute those numbers.
  • Wrapping each lookup in its own agent added model calls, waiting time and repeated tool calls, without adding judgment.

My conclusion. Campus Customs would be better served by one capable operations agent holding all the read tools. It would gather the facts for every ticket, read stock, vendors, leases and cash once, and handle routine obligations like ticket 102 by itself. It should delegate to specialists only when a ticket needs a distinct judgment or a distinct kind of output:

  • Accounting for any proposal that spends money or changes a price, including purchase orders, so affordability is always checked.
  • Customer Service for anything a customer will read.

The safety rules should stay where they actually live, in the backend and MCP layer: exact amounts, human approval, vendor rechecks and the cash floor. They protect the shop whichever design sits on top.

4. Three new problems the current team could not solve

I checked these gaps against what exists: the 11 registered MCP tools and the 12 tables in the working database (desk, inventory, pricing, vendors, invoices, leases, cash_accounts, payments, tickets, plus the system's ticket_runs, action_proposals and approvals).

Problem 1 — "Will our 12-hoodie order actually arrive, and when do we pay for it?"

A. The problem. A week after approving the 103 order, the operator needs three answers: did Bulldog Print Co ship the 12 hoodies, when will they arrive, and when is the $264 bill due? The team can partly investigate. check_vendor_eligibility still gives the vendor's lead_days, so it can say "about 5 days if ordered on the shop date". It cannot complete the task, because nothing in the system knows the order was placed, shipped or billed.

B. What's missing:

  • Approved purchase orders exist only as action_proposals rows with status approved. There is no order record, no vendor integration that sends the order, and no shipment-tracking data.
  • There is no tool that adds received goods to inventory.qty.
  • There is no payables record of the future $264 bill, or the $8 one from ticket 101.

C. What would be needed:

  • New tables: a purchase_orders table (vendor, SKU, size, quantity, cost, status, expected date) and a payables table.
  • New tools: a supplier or carrier integration with a get_shipment_status(po_id) tool, and a receive_goods(po_id) write tool that increases inventory and opens the vendor's invoice.
  • Ownership: Inventory should own tracking and receiving, and Accounting should own the resulting invoice. I don't think this needs a new agent.
  • Safeguards:
    • receive_goods should require a human to confirm the physical count, so agents never adjust stock unseen.
    • Sending an order to a vendor should require a separate human approval, distinct from today's "approve as draft".

D. Connection to the tickets: both 101 and 103 ended with approved orders that were never sent, and the Cash tab notes $272 of commitments that appear nowhere in the database. That gap is where real money will eventually go missing.

Problem 2 — "Can we afford October, and should we have bought those hoodies?"

A. The problem. The owner asks whether the shop will be able to pay October's $2,400 rent, and whether the 103 restock was a good bet. The team can partly answer: it can show $160 in checking, $2,400 due 2026-10-02, and the hoodie margin. It cannot answer the real question, because the system has no idea what money is coming in.

B. What's missing:

  • There is no sales, receipts or revenue data of any kind; cash only flows out by design.
  • There is no sales history to forecast demand. Nothing says whether the Yale AI Club will confirm, or how fast size M hoodies sell.

C. What would be needed:

  • Data: read access to a point-of-sale or online-store sales feed, as a sales table (date, SKU, size, quantity, price, channel) and a cash_receipts feed.
  • New tools: get_sales_history(sku, period) and a forecast_cash(horizon_days) tool combining expected receipts with the obligations that get_cash_and_obligations already lists.
  • A new agent: here I think a Planning or Forecasting agent is justified. Forecasting is a distinct skill with its own data and failure modes, such as seasonality around move-in and graduation. It would answer to the Boss and give Accounting projected cash before any big purchase.
  • Safeguards: it should be read-only, and its forecasts should be labelled as estimates. The payment tool must keep checking actual cash, never forecast cash.

D. Connection to the tickets: in 103 I approved a $264 order against $160 in checking on the assumption that the club's $1,044 order would come through. The data didn't support or refute that. Before Problem 9, Accounting also kept escalating because it couldn't tell whether next month's rent was a real threat. Both point to this missing view of incoming cash.

Problem 3 — "Please email Tauhid and hold the club's hoodies"

A. The problem. The operator approves the 101 and 103 replies and wants them actually sent, and wants the 8 hoodies the club asked about held so they aren't sold to someone else. The team can write the messages, as it already did in 101 and 103, and it can confirm the 8 hoodies exist. It cannot send anything, reserve stock or know how to reach the customers.

B. What's missing:

  • There is no messaging tool at all, by design.
  • There is no customer contact data. tickets.requester is only a name.
  • There is no reservations or orders table, and no tool that writes inventory.
  • Customer drafts aren't even stored as approvable proposals; they live only in the run result.

C. What would be needed:

  • Data: a customers table with contact details and consent, and a reservations table.
  • New tools: reserve_stock(sku, size, qty, ticket_id), which decrements available stock and is reversible, and a send_message(draft_id) tool through an email or SMS provider.
  • Ownership: Customer Service keeps ownership. I wouldn't add a new agent, but I would store each draft as a proposal so the operator approves the exact text.
  • Safeguards:
    • send_message should only run on a human-approved, unchanged draft, the same way approve_proposal works for money.
    • reserve_stock should expire automatically.

D. Connection to the tickets: both 101 and 103 "resolved" while their customer replies were still sitting as drafts that nobody could approve or send. From the shop's side the ticket was closed; from the customer's side nothing had happened. That mismatch is the clearest boundary between what I built and what a real operations desk needs.

5. What the Cash tab tells me

The ledger is simple, and that's partly why I trust it. Checking was $3,400.00 after the reset. Only two payments ever moved cash:

  • Payment 1: $840.00 for invoice 501 (ticket 101).
  • Payment 2: $2,400.00 for rent on lease 1 (ticket 102).

That is $3,240.00 in total, leaving $160.00. It matches SUM(payments.amount), cash_accounts.balance, the API and the dashboard. Ticket 103 moved no cash. The other three approvals (#2, the $8 tee order; #4, the $264 hoodie order; #5, the discount) were real human decisions, but none of them is a payment, and the Cash tab correctly records $0.00 for them.

Financial constraints shaped the decisions in visible ways:

  • In 101, the agents paid the overdue invoice only after Accounting showed it left the rent covered.
  • In 102, the rent payment was approved knowing it left just $160.
  • In 103, the tight cash should have shaped the order decision, but no agent checked it.

Was the approval process appropriate and enforced? Yes, within its design:

  • Every payment was proposed by an agent, approved explicitly in the dashboard, and executed by the backend only after it re-checked the invoice or lease and the balance.
  • Each approval had to repeat the exact amount and payee.
  • Duplicate approvals would have been refused.

I want to be honest about two things, though:

  • The approvals in this run were entered by Claude Code at my direction, recorded as "Celia Gehant (entered by Claude Code)". The backend has no authentication, so approved_by is only as trustworthy as whoever types it.
  • The insufficient-cash guard was never triggered in Problem 9, because every payment was affordable. I've seen it work only in the offline tests (a $500 payment against $160 was refused with the balance unchanged), not on these three tickets.

What the run revealed:

  • Unnecessary steps: Accounting repeating Inventory's lookups in 101, and Accounting re-reading cash that Facilities already had in 102.
  • Missing checks: no affordability review of the $264 order in 103, and nowhere to record the $272 of approved orders as liabilities.

A similar future invoice problem would be handled well today. A new overdue invoice from Elm City Gifts, for example, would be found by get_cash_and_obligations and check_vendor_eligibility, proposed at its exact amount, and paid only on approval, with the cash floor enforced by the database trigger. October's rent (Problem 2 in section 3) would correctly be refused for insufficient funds.

For anything more complex — scheduling payments against expected income, partial payments, vendor credit terms, or the eventual bills for approved orders — the system would need:

  • a payables table and purchase-order records
  • a revenue or receipts feed
  • vendor payment terms (the vendors table has none)
  • a cash-forecast tool

None of those exist yet, and I'd rather say so than let a clean $160 closing balance suggest the shop's finances are fully under control.

What I'm taking away

The most valuable parts of this project turned out to be the least glamorous:

  • the rule that every cited record must come from a tool
  • the exact-amount check
  • the vendor recheck at approval time
  • the cash floor in the database
  • the append-only audit trail that let me reconstruct every run

The agents were genuinely useful where judgment was needed — weighing the invoice against rent, framing the discount, writing to customers. They were less useful as couriers of facts. And the hardest lesson came from my own Expected plans and earlier runs. A system can "resolve" a ticket in the database while the customer is still waiting, and while an approved order is quietly committing cash the shop doesn't have. Knowing where that line sits matters more to me now than any single resolved ticket.
